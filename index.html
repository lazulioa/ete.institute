CASE 001 · IR-2019-11 · recomputed from public telemetry

verified event

On 16 November 2019, following fuel-price protests, Iranian authorities ordered a shutdown: cellular operators disconnected first around 14:00 local, and by 19:00 the country entered digital darkness — international connectivity down to roughly 4–5%. The near-total blackout held until 10:00 on 21 November: 111 hours. During the window, security forces killed at least 304 people; the domestic intranet stayed online. The event is cross-verified by four independent sources: IODA (BGP / active probing / telescope signals), OONI (measurements still arriving from 49 networks — proof the blackout was not total), Google traffic data, and the Amnesty International × Hertie School forensic investigation.

schematic reconstruction anchored to documented signal levels (100% → ~4–5% → recovery); not the raw series. sources: ooni.org/post/2019-iran-internet-blackout · ioda.inetintel.cc.gatech.edu · iran-shutdown.amnesty.org · pulse.internetsociety.org

CASE 002 · TO-2022-01 · single-point fragility

verified event

On 15 January 2022, the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai eruption severed Tonga's only international subsea cable (~827 km to Fiji), cutting nearly all internet connectivity for a population of ~105,000. Mainline repair took about five weeks, restored 22 February; limited satellite links carried government traffic in between. Compiled constraint: critical connectivity requires redundant paths (ITU resilience guidance) — membership test: FAILED — one cable, no redundancy. The case shows fragility becoming a measurable governance fact without any actor violating anything.

Research — one question, four laboratories

How can computational systems remain understandable, contestable and governable as societies shift from legal text to algorithmic infrastructure? Each laboratory holds one face of that question.

The institute is organized as four laboratories, not a collection of disconnected projects. Each lab holds one face of the question: whether geometry can carry governance (Lab 1); whether law can be computed and stay contestable (Lab 2); whether industry and the built environment can answer for their obligations (Lab 3); and whether institutions themselves can be designed and experimented upon (Lab 4). Prototype stages, snapshot data and asserted invariants are labeled on the instrument surface itself.

LAB 1

Geometrical Governance

spatial composition · organizational geometry · topology · satellite systems

Problem: the objects of governance are spatial — orbital slots, spectrum, low-altitude corridors, organizational structures. Method: compile them into checkable geometry: composition, topology, the satellite layer. Instruments: the Landscape Instrument v8 · the Composer & green slope · the trust polytope · the six-window front instrument.

LAB 2

Computational Law

ai auditing · legal complexity · contestability · infrastructure compliance

Problem: obligations are written for humans but enforced against computational systems. Method: compile law into executable audit geometry — compliance as membership test, every breach a signed record. Instruments: the Governance Graph prototype · the trust engine & forensic loop · DSA/AI-Act/CSDDD/co-determination schema pilots · the registry.

LAB 3

Industrial Intelligence

bim · digital twins · construction ai · infrastructure governance

Problem: twins that represent infrastructure do not answer for it. Method: build twins that do — IFC in, codes compiled, agents audit, signed compliance out; the bilingual EU + GB standards library is a capability no one else holds. Current work: the BIM/twin compliance pilot · the OUIM & ISH engines · Beijing BIM-AI field research · AMYGDALA, the encrypted edge-sentinel robotics unit (concept: spatial recognition and alerting that only stores and decodes signals — a witness, never a controller; non-military by design).

LAB 4

Institution Design

collective structures · governance experiments

Problem: institutions are usually inherited, not designed. Method: design collective structures and run governance experiments on them — no slot without a socket, one sandbox case per organization, the three-monies firewall, the never-for-sale registry. Current experiments: the Terra Forum & its three sockets · the Bluebook open assembly · the Sandbox · the institute's own governance model.

LAB 5

Cultural Forensics

memorial culture · cybernetic mourning · archival refusal · anthropological forensics

Problem: memory institutions store, but can they mourn? Method: cultural and anthropological forensics — memorial culture, cybernetic mourning, practices of archival refusal; storing what is most human in the most humanless space, and asking how a system ontologically unable to mourn could translate mourning. Current work: the Ritual Memory Datacenter — a memorial drafted collectively.

About — an independent institute for computational governance

We do not publish opinions about auditability — we publish instruments, and the signed records they produce.

ÉTÈ Institute (Eternal Terra Ear · 永地耳) is an independent research institute for computational governance, fiscally hosted by Open Source Europe in Brussels, working bilingually in English and Chinese across Brussels, Berlin and Beijing. We hold one thesis: as governance migrates from legal text to algorithmic infrastructure, it remains legitimate only if verification is constructive.

Commitment 01

Constructive verification

Every claim we make about a system is backed by a model a third party can run.

Commitment 02

Evidence over assertion

Outputs are cryptographically signed, hash-anchored and archived; methodology published in the open. Our results do not require trusting us.

Commitment 03

Contestability by design

The communities monitored by a model must be able to inspect, recompute and challenge it — a design requirement, not an ethics annex.

Investment enters only the service entity — never the protocol or registry, which are held for the commons in perpetuity. The registry is not for sale; that sentence is our governance model. Dual-use here means investigative and commercial — never military.

RITUAL.MEMORY.DATACENTER · NECROPOLIS.INDEX · v0 · 永土耳

Mortality salience is a documented driver of human information behavior: burial, inscription, archiving and transmission operate as loss-mitigation systems. This experiment tests a hypothesis — whether affective content can be preserved in infrastructures that lack affect, and what is lost in the translation.

Method — Corpus: documentary footage of funerals, mourning and burial rites. Index structure: a spatialized necropolis topology (traversable, enterable chambers). Interaction protocol: traverse, focus, step inside. Metadata schema: time, place, rite type per death event. The system is designed to keep operating unattended — not as metaphor but as experimental condition.

RQ 1

Storage ≠ mourning

The act of storing derives from loss aversion and is therefore a human trait; the storage infrastructure itself does not participate in mourning. Distinguishing the two is the experiment's first measurement.

RQ 2

Machine thanatology

Technical systems have their own termination modes: expired credentials, unresolvable links, decommissioned servers, unreadable formats. Operational death can be formally defined and indexed — the experiment places it beside the metadata of biological death.

RQ 3

Limits of translation

A data center preserves traces of death through technical operations — metadata, redundancy, checksums — rather than ritual: preservation without mourning. The core question is thus statable precisely: which properties of mourning can a system ontologically unable to mourn preserve, and which are lost?

Case method — Practices of archival refusal as research object: refusing what has been refused to us; anti-generalisation; working in case series and essays. The series spans successive revolutionary periods: grassroots archives created, appropriated, and reconstructed.

Theoretical ground · Ruffini & Castaldo, Pattern, Persist! (2026)

Algorithmic persistence: a compressed identity that survives the filter of time

Our agents follow the algorithmic agent's triad — a Modeling Engine, a scalar Objective Function, a Planning Engine: model-mediated regulation. Every C2PA manifest is exactly that "compressed identity"; every boundary check is an irreversible act of regulation across a thin, thermodynamically costly boundary (its Landauer cost is the price of trust). By the Algorithmic Regulator Theorem, a regulator shares mutual algorithmic information with what it regulates — an auditor must contain a model of the audited. This is why the evidence must persist with the instrument. (external reference: Ruffini & Castaldo, Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.21278422, CC-BY 4.0 — not an ÉTÈ publication)
